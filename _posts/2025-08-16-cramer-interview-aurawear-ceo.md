### On AI in Creative Design

**Jim Cramer:** Architecting it! I love that! That's ownership! That’s what I want from a CEO! Let's get specific. I walk into your design studio in Milan. It's full of artists, creatives... people who *feel* fashion. Aschenbrenner's world suggests a machine could do that. How do you replace the human soul in a cashmere sweater design? Tell me!

**Sarah Chen:** You don't replace it, Jim. You amplify it. Right now, our designers spend maybe 30% of their time on pure creative ideation. The other 70% is spent on things like generating 2D technical sketches, creating colorway variations, building spec sheets, and rendering photorealistic models for internal review. It's necessary but time-consuming work.

Now, imagine this: Our lead designer describes a concept—"a London fog-inspired trench coat, 1960s silhouette, with a futuristic, sustainable fabric." An AI agent instantly generates a dozen photorealistic, wearable 3D models. It also produces the technical sketches, identifies potential supply chain partners for the sustainable fabric, and even drafts initial marketing copy. Our designer goes from being a draftsperson to a creative director, curating and refining at lightning speed. Their creative output doesn't go down; it goes up by a factor of 10. Time-to-market for a new concept could go from 6 months to 6 weeks.

### Transforming the Back Office

**Jim Cramer:** A factor of **TEN!** That’s a number I can get behind! That’s margin expansion! That’s competitive advantage! *(SOUND of a "BUY, BUY, BUY" button)* But what about the back office? The less glamorous stuff. Your accountants, your HR department, your data analysts. Are they safe?

**Sarah Chen:** They aren't safe from change, but they are positioned for evolution. This is where the McKinsey data is really compelling. Their latest research suggests generative AI could automate business activities that absorb 60 to 70 percent of an employee's time today. We're already seeing it.

In finance, our teams now use AI agents to conduct initial audits, build complex forecast models, and analyze expense reports for anomalies in seconds. Our human analysts don't spend their days in spreadsheets; they spend their days strategizing based on the AI's output. They ask bigger questions.

In marketing, we used to have a team of 20 people manually analyzing social media trends. Now, an AI model does that in real-time and can predict micro-trends before they peak. It then drafts dozens of targeted ad campaigns, and our marketing strategists choose the best ones. Productivity in that department is already up 40% in our pilot programs.

### The Human Element and the Future Workforce

**Jim Cramer:** Forty percent! Are you kidding me?! That’s not incremental! That's a new paradigm! So, you're building a company of managers and directors who oversee an army of digital workers. Is that the endgame? What happens to the junior analyst, the entry-level coder?

**Sarah Chen:** That's the critical human question, Jim. The nature of the "junior" role is changing. An entry-level coder won't be writing boilerplate code; they'll be directing an AI coding assistant like Devin or a similar internal model to build and debug entire applications. Their job becomes about system architecture and prompt engineering, not syntax. The new "entry-level" skill isn't knowing a specific coding language; it's the ability to articulate a complex problem to an AI.

We are investing massively in reskilling. We're creating roles that didn't exist two years ago: "AI-Human Interaction Designers," "Supply Chain AI Ethicists," "Generative Brand Stewards." The repetitive, cognitive-lite tasks will be automated. The jobs that remain will be more creative, more strategic, and frankly, more engaging. But it requires a commitment to retraining, and that's a commitment we're making.
